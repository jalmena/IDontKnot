### Systems where the user manipulates a rope and the result is checked

Only research systems were found. No consumer product turned up.

- **Brown, Latombe and Montgomery, "Real-time knot-tying simulation"** (The Visual Computer, 2004). The user grasps a simulated rope and ties knots, including around objects, and a module recognises which knot has formed. This is the closest prior art.
- **KnotPad** (IEEE VIS 2012). Manipulation of 2D mathematical knot diagrams in which every drag is a valid Reidemeister move. No 3D rope and no practical knots.
- **Surgical knot-tying simulators.** Physics-based thread at haptic rates; they score a surgical task and do not recognise named knots.

Consumer games and VR applications were not searched.

### The gap

Every consumer product is passive: watch, rotate, follow a video. Systems that let a user manipulate a rope are research prototypes for mathematics, surgery or robotics. Nothing found lets a user tie a named practical knot on a virtual rope, checks the result, and connects it to sourced documentation, across several disciplines. That is the space this product aims at. The search was not exhaustive, so "nothing exists" is probable, not proven.

## Content provenance

| Source | Reusable? | Conditions and traps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wikipedia text | Yes, CC BY-SA | Attribution (title, link to history, licence). Derivatives must stay CC BY-SA, so imported text must be kept separable from our own. |
| Wikimedia Commons files | Per file | Every file has its own licence. Check each one. |
| Wikidata | Yes, CC0 | A property for Ashley Book of Knots numbers exists (reported as P1806; confirm on wikidata.org). |
| The Ashley Book of Knots (1944) | **Status unverified** | In the United States it is public domain only if the copyright was not renewed; renewal was not checked. In the EU, Ashley died in 1947, so life plus 70 years has passed (*unverified as a legal conclusion*). The 1993 revised edition has newer material. **Do not reproduce its text or drawings.** |
| ABOK numbers as references | Generally accepted | They are identifiers, used throughout Wikipedia and Wikidata. |
| International Guild of Knot Tyers | Treat as all rights reserved | *Unverified*; their site was not checked. |
| United States Army manuals | Public domain as federal works | TC 3-97.61 Military Mountaineering (2012) and its predecessors. Third-party images inside a manual may still be copyrighted. Use official copies. |
| Books published before 1931 | Public domain in the United States | Reprints and redrawn plates carry new copyright. Use scans of the original. |

Working rules that follow:

1. Text and illustrations are written and drawn for this project unless a source is under a licence that permits reuse, in which case the licence and attribution travel with the content.
2. Facts may be cited from any source. Expression may not be copied from sources that reserve their rights.
3. Every content item records its provenance and licence in its metadata.
4. No proprietary application database, paid course or commercial illustration is copied.

## Safety sources by discipline

| Discipline | Source | Can be cited for | Cannot be cited for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Climbing, rope access | HSE Contract Research Report 364/2001 (Lyon Equipment) | Strength of termination knots under specified tests on the ropes of that era | Current practice, other ropes |
| Climbing | Manufacturer technical notices (for example Petzl, Edelrid) | Behaviour of specific knots on specific products; ranges of strength loss | General standards |
| Climbing | UIAA standards | Equipment requirements | Knot technique; no UIAA knot standard was found (*unverified*) |
| Nautical | Milne and McLaren (2006), strength of knots and splices in sailing ropes | Comparative break tests | Safe working loads |
| Arboriculture | ANSI Z133 (2026 edition announced by the ISA) | Safety requirements for arboricultural work | Knot-level technique |
| Pioneering | Scout associations' activity rules | Organisational rules where they can be read at source | Height limits: the commonly quoted figures could not be confirmed at source |
| Treenets and other rope structures that carry people | **No standard was found** | Nothing | Any statement that a structure is safe |
| Shibari | Khodulev and others, "Acute radial compressive neuropathy: the most common injury induced by Japanese rope bondage" (Cureus, 2023) | Radial nerve injury as the most frequent injury, mostly in suspension | Incidence rates |
| Shibari | Community safety resources and consent frameworks | Practice guidance, clearly labelled as community recommendations | Medical claims |

Consequences for the product:

- Every safety-relevant statement carries an evidence class: established fact, manufacturer specification, experimental observation, recommendation, or unverified.
- Strength figures are quoted only with their source, the rope tested and the test method. None are computed or invented.
- Treenets and suspension content state plainly that no standard exists.
- Shibari content includes consent, circulation and nerve-compression information as part of the knot's documentation, not as a footnote.
